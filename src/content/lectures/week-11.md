Every studio so far has had its author in the room, ready to say "try moving
the pointer faster" or "hold the key down." An exhibition removes that
person. What's left has to teach itself in the first few seconds, or it
teaches the viewer nothing at all and they move on. That's a much stricter bar
than "works when I use it," and it's also a different kind of testing: running
an instrument unattended for two minutes and seeing what state it's actually
in surfaces failure modes --- a listener that never unregisters, a decay term
that reaches a stuck boundary and stays there --- that never show up in a
five-minute demo where a human keeps intervening.

Staging is the other half: what does a viewer need physically (a mouse, a
touchscreen, both hands free) and what, if anything, do they need told before
they touch it. The right amount of instruction is usually smaller than its
author expects.

## Outline

- unattended operation as its own test, distinct from "it works when I demo it"
- failure modes that only surface after minutes of no input, not seconds
- staging: what a viewer needs to be told, and how to make that as little as possible

## Look at

Nina Simon's *The Participatory Museum* is written for exhibit designers
staging interactives for visitors who arrive with no docent and no
instruction sheet, which is the exact problem this lecture is about --- her
running theme, that the best label is the one a visitor never has to read,
is a good test to hold your own instrument to next week.
