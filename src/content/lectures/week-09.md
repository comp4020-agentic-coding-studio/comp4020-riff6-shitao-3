Generative art has a long history of keeping the failure and throwing away
the polish: Vera Molnár's plotter drawings lean on the machine doing something
slightly other than commanded, and circuit-bent and glitched instruments treat
a malfunction as a found sound rather than a defect to route around. None of
that is licence to ship whatever breaks first. The test this course uses is
narrow: can you say, in one sentence, what a viewer is meant to feel when the
failure happens, and is that better than the instrument simply working? If the
answer is no, it's a bug, and the fix is the same as it would be in any other
week --- not a story you tell about it afterwards.

The reason this comes in week 9 rather than week 1 is deliberate: you need six
weeks of actually debugging real accidental failures first, so that "this
break is deliberate" is a claim you can back up by contrast, not a cover story
for something you didn't get around to fixing.

## Outline

- generative art's own history of kept, not fixed, malfunctions
- the one-sentence test for "deliberate" versus "still a bug"
- why this lecture comes after six weeks of real debugging, not before it

## Look at

Reed Ghazala, who coined "circuit bending" after a toy synthesiser
short-circuited into a sound nobody had designed, is the clearest real-world
version of this week's test: the discovery was an accident, but every bent
circuit he built and sold afterwards was a deliberate, repeatable choice
about which accidents were worth keeping. That's the distance between
finding a failure and defending one.
